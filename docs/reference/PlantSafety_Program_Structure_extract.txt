Preface
Manufacturing plants operate in environments where safety is not optional—it is foundational. Every machine, process, chemical, and human interaction introduces potential hazards that must be identified, controlled, and continuously monitored. PlantSafety was created to meet this challenge head
on.
This application is designed to be a comprehensive safety management system that empowers facilities to understand their risks, comply with regulatory requirements, and protect their workforce. While many safety tools focus on minimal functionality, PlantSafety intentionally takes a broader, more detailed approach. The goal is to provide users with all relevant information without drifting into unnecessary complexity or overwhelming the workflow.
The foundation of PlantSafety has been built using C# and SQL Server to ensure reliability, scalability, and long
term maintainability. With this solid base in place, we are now entering the feature
expansion phase—adding modules that cover every aspect of manufacturing plant safety, including compliance, employee protection, contractor oversight, machine safeguarding, emergency response, risk management, and more.
This is an ongoing development effort. As manufacturing environments evolve, so will PlantSafety. New features, deeper integrations, and expanded data models will be added to ensure the system remains aligned with OSHA standards, industry best practices, and emerging technologies such as IoT
based monitoring and AI
driven hazard detection.
PlantSafety is not just a tool—it is a strategic safety partner designed to help organizations reduce incidents, improve training, streamline audits, and maintain a culture where safety is prioritized at every level.
Executive Summary
 
PlantSafety is a comprehensive, enterprise
grade software platform designed to help manufacturing facilities proactively manage workplace safety, regulatory compliance, and operational risk. Built on a robust C# and SQL Server foundation, the system integrates every major component of modern plant safety—from OSHA compliance and machine guarding to contractor oversight, emergency response, and real
time observability.
Manufacturing environments face increasing pressure from regulatory agencies, insurance carriers, and internal stakeholders to reduce incidents, improve training, and maintain airtight documentation. PlantSafety addresses these challenges by centralizing safety data, automating compliance workflows, and providing actionable insights through dashboards, scorecards, and audit tools.
The platform supports both leading indicators (training completion, inspections, hazard identification) and lagging indicators (TRIR, DART, lost workdays) to give organizations a complete picture of their safety performance. It also incorporates advanced modules such as IoT
based observability, permit
to
work systems, contractor vetting, and predictive maintenance scheduling.
PlantSafety’s ultimate mission is twofold:
Protect employees and equipment through proactive hazard management.
Ensure continuous compliance with OSHA, EPA, state, federal, and industry
specific regulations.
As development progresses, PlantSafety will evolve into a fully integrated safety ecosystem capable of supporting large
scale manufacturing operations with precision, clarity, and reliability.
Core Compliance Components
1. Workplace Hazard Protocols
Strict controls must be implemented to manage high
risk activities and equipment. This includes:
Safe operation of powered industrial trucks (forklifts, pallet jacks, AGVs)
Fall protection systems for elevated work areas
Electrical safety protocols, including arc
flash boundaries and equipment labeling
Guarding of pinch points, rotating parts, and high
energy machinery
These protocols ensure that workers operate equipment safely and that hazardous zones are clearly identified and controlled.
2. Occupational Health & PPE Compliance
Facilities must mandate, supply, and routinely test Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) to ensure proper protection against:
Hazardous noise levels
Respiratory threats (dust, fumes, vapors)
Chemical exposure
Heat, cold, and mechanical hazards
Compliance includes fit
testing, PPE inspections, replacement schedules, and documentation of employee training on proper use.
3. Emergency Preparedness & Response
A compliant facility maintains clear, accessible, and regularly updated emergency plans, including:
Evacuation routes and muster points
Fire hazard mitigation and extinguisher placement
Spill response procedures
Incident response documentation
Routine emergency drills
These measures ensure employees know exactly how to respond during fires, chemical spills, severe weather, or medical emergencies.
4. Physical Security & Access Control
Compliance extends beyond internal hazards to include facility security measures such as:
Restricted access to hazardous zones
Surveillance systems and perimeter monitoring
Controlled entry for contractors and visitors
Secure storage of chemicals, tools, and high
risk equipment
These controls reduce unauthorized access and protect both personnel and assets.
5. Training, Documentation & Auditing
A compliant safety program requires continuous education and verification, including:
Mandatory onboarding safety training for all employees and contractors
Specialized training for workers handling new equipment or hazardous materials
Routine facility inspections and safety audits
Documented corrective actions and follow
up verification
Maintenance of OSHA
required records (injuries, incidents, SDS, training logs)
Training and auditing ensure that safety protocols are understood, followed, and continuously improved.
Employee Safety
 
Employee safety in manufacturing environments is the foundation of a successful and compliant operation. It encompasses hazard identification, OSHA compliance, machine guarding, Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) protocols, ergonomic design, chemical management, and comprehensive emergency response planning. A strong employee safety program not only protects workers but also reduces downtime, improves morale, and ensures regulatory compliance.
A robust employee safety framework integrates engineering controls, administrative policies, training programs, and continuous monitoring to prevent injuries and maintain a safe 
working environment. It requires collaboration between management, supervisors, safety professionals, and frontline employees to identify hazards early and implement corrective actions quickly.
Core Components of Employee Safety
1. Machine Guarding
Machine guarding prevents workers from contacting hazardous moving parts, pinch points, sharp edges, and high
energy mechanisms. Effective guarding includes:
Fixed guards and barrier systems
Interlocked doors that disable machine operation when opened
Adjustable guards for tools and cutting equipment
Safety interlocks and presence
sensing devices
These controls ensure that workers cannot enter danger zones during machine operation.
2. Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) Procedures
LOTO protocols protect employees during maintenance, repair, or cleaning by ensuring machines remain fully de
energized. A compliant LOTO program includes:
Identification of all energy sources (electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, chemical)
Use of locks, hasps, and tags to secure isolation points
Verification steps to confirm zero
energy state
Authorized personnel training and annual program audits
LOTO prevents accidental startup and is one of the most critical OSHA requirements in manufacturing.
3. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)
PPE serves as the final line of defense against workplace hazards. Facilities must provide, maintain, and enforce the use of:
Hard hats and bump caps
Safety glasses, goggles, and face shields
Cut
resistant, chemical
resistant, or heat
resistant gloves
Hearing protection (earplugs, earmuffs)
Steel
toe or composite
toe boots
High
visibility vests and flame
resistant clothing
PPE programs include fit
testing, inspections, replacement schedules, and training on proper use.
4. Ergonomics & Material Handling
Ergonomic safety reduces musculoskeletal injuries caused by repetitive motion, awkward posture, or heavy lifting. Key elements include:
Adjustable workstations and ergonomic tools
Mechanical lifting aids (hoists, conveyors, carts)
Training on proper lifting techniques
Job rotation to reduce repetitive strain
Evaluations of workstation layout and workflow
These measures help prevent sprains, strains, and long
term musculoskeletal disorders.
5. Hazard Communication & Training
Employees must be fully informed about workplace hazards and trained to work safely. A compliant HazCom program includes:
Clear labeling of hazardous chemicals using GHS standards
Accessible Safety Data Sheets (SDS)
Training on chemical handling, storage, and emergency response
Instruction on safe machine operation and hazard recognition
Routine safety audits and hazard reporting systems
Effective communication ensures workers understand risks and know how to mitigate them.
6. Emergency Preparedness
Preparedness ensures employees can respond quickly and correctly during emergencies. Essential components include:
Clearly marked evacuation routes and exits
Emergency stop buttons on machinery
Fire safety protocols and extinguisher placement
Spill response kits and chemical containment procedures
Regular emergency drills and alarm testing
These systems protect employees during fires, spills, severe weather, or medical emergencies.
7. Environmental & Health Monitoring
Environmental controls protect employees from airborne, noise, and vibration hazards. Monitoring programs typically include:
Ventilation systems and air
quality sensors
Dust collection and filtration systems
Noise level assessments and hearing conservation programs
Vibration exposure monitoring for tools and equipment
Respiratory protection programs for airborne contaminants
Continuous monitoring ensures that environmental conditions remain within safe limits.
Contractor Safety Management
 
Contractor safety management in manufacturing environments ensures that third
party workers operate safely, comply with facility rules, and do not introduce new hazards into the plant. Because contractors often perform high
risk tasks—maintenance, repairs, construction, electrical work, confined space entry—their activities must be tightly controlled, documented, and monitored. A strong contractor safety program protects employees, contractors, equipment, and the organization’s regulatory standing.
Effective contractor management standardizes the entire lifecycle of external workers: pre
qualification, onboarding, permit authorization, active oversight, and post
job evaluation. This reduces liability, prevents incidents, and ensures contractors meet the same safety expectations as full
time employees.
Core Components of Contractor Safety Management
1. Pre
Qualification & Vetting
Before contractors enter the facility, they must be screened to ensure they meet safety and compliance standards. This process includes:
Reviewing safety performance metrics such as 
Experience Modification Rate (EMR)
 and 
Total Recordable Incident Rate (TRIR)
Verifying certifications, licenses, and insurance coverage
Confirming OSHA training compliance (e.g., 10
hour or 30
hour courses)
Assessing historical incident records and corrective action responsiveness
Evaluating the contractor’s internal safety policies and training programs
This vetting ensures only qualified, safety
focused contractors are approved for work.
2. Site
Specific Orientation
Contractors must receive training tailored to the facility’s unique hazards and operational environment. Orientation typically includes:
Plant layout, restricted zones, and designated walkways
Emergency alarm signals, evacuation routes, and muster points
Chemical hazards, SDS access, and spill response expectations
Machine guarding rules and proximity hazards
PPE requirements for specific areas or tasks
Communication protocols with supervisors and safety personnel
This ensures contractors understand the facility’s risks and expectations before beginning work.
3. Permit
to
Work (PTW) Systems
High
risk tasks require formal authorization through a structured permit system. Common permit types include:
Hot Work Permits
 (welding, cutting, grinding)
Confined Space Entry Permits
 (tanks, pits, silos)
Electrical / Isolation Permits
 (LOTO procedures, energized work)
Working at Height Permits
 (scaffolding, elevated platforms)
Excavation Permits
 (trenching, underground utilities)
Each permit documents:
Scope and location of work
Hazard assessments and required controls
PPE requirements
Atmospheric testing results (for confined spaces)
Authorization signatures and validity periods
PTW systems ensure hazardous work is planned, controlled, and monitored.
4. Active Monitoring & Auditing
Once contractors begin work, continuous oversight is essential. Monitoring activities include:
Routine site visits and safety observations
Verification of PPE compliance
Inspection of tools, equipment, and LOTO devices
Ensuring adherence to permit conditions
Immediate correction of unsafe behaviors or conditions
Documentation of findings for audit trails
Active monitoring ensures contractors maintain safe practices throughout the job.
5. Incident Tracking & Performance Evaluation
If an incident, near
miss, or unsafe condition occurs, contractors must be included in the facility’s incident management process. This includes:
Conducting investigations and root
cause analysis
Documenting corrective and preventive actions (CAPA)
Reviewing contractor response and cooperation
Assessing overall safety performance at project completion
Determining eligibility for future work based on safety behavior
This evaluation helps facilities build a trusted pool of contractors with proven safety records.
Machine Safety & Protective Systems
 
Machine safety in manufacturing environments is essential for preventing severe injuries, equipment damage, and regulatory violations. Modern plants rely on engineered controls—guards, interlocks, sensors, and emergency systems—to protect workers from moving parts, pinch points, stored energy, and hazardous materials. A robust machine safety program integrates physical safeguards, automated detection systems, and strict Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) protocols to ensure machines operate safely throughout their lifecycle.
Machine safety is not limited to equipment design; it also includes routine inspections, preventive maintenance, operator training, and continuous monitoring. Together, these elements create a controlled environment where workers can operate machinery confidently and safely.
Core Machine Safety Systems
1. Machine Guards & Barriers
Machine guards are the first line of defense against mechanical hazards. Effective guarding includes:
Fixed guards
 that permanently block access to dangerous moving parts
Interlocked doors
 that automatically stop machine operation when opened
Physical enclosures
 around robotic cells, cutting tools, and high
speed equipment
Adjustable guards
 for tools that require operator positioning
These barriers prevent accidental contact with gears, blades, belts, rollers, and other hazardous components.
2. Emergency Stop (E
Stop) Devices
Emergency stop systems allow workers to immediately halt machine motion during unsafe conditions. Common E
Stop devices include:
Large, red 
push
button stops
 located near operator stations
Pull
cord systems
 along conveyor lines
Cable
actuated stops
 for long or complex machinery
E
Stops must be easily accessible, clearly marked, and tested regularly to ensure reliable operation.
3. Presence
Sensing Devices
Advanced sensing technologies automatically stop machinery when a worker enters a danger zone. These include:
Light curtains
 that detect interruptions in infrared beams
Safety laser scanners
 that monitor defined protective fields
Pressure
sensitive mats
 that trigger shutdown when stepped on
Presence
sensing devices are essential for robotic work cells, automated assembly lines, and high
speed equipment.
4. Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) Systems
LOTO systems isolate hazardous energy sources during maintenance, cleaning, or repair. Key components include:
Padlocks, hasps, and tags
 for securing electrical switches, valves, and breakers
Energy isolation points
 clearly labeled and documented
Verification procedures
 to confirm zero
energy state
Authorized personnel training
 and annual audits
LOTO prevents accidental machine startup and is one of the most critical OSHA requirements for equipment safety.
5. Two
Hand Controls
Two
hand control systems require operators to press two separate buttons simultaneously to activate machine cycles. This ensures:
Hands remain outside the danger zone
Operators cannot inadvertently reach into hazardous areas
Machines only cycle when both controls are engaged
These systems are commonly used on presses, cutters, and forming machines.

 that monitor critical circuits

Safety Procedures
 
Manufacturing plant safety procedures are the documented, repeatable protocols that ensure workers perform tasks safely, consistently, and in compliance with regulatory standards. These procedures form the backbone of a facility’s safety management system, guiding employees through hazard identification, equipment operation, emergency response, and daily workplace practices. When properly implemented, safety procedures reduce incidents, improve operational reliability, and create a culture where safe behavior is the norm.
A robust safety procedure framework integrates engineering controls, administrative rules, PPE requirements, and continuous training. It ensures that every worker—whether new, experienced, or contracted—understands how to perform tasks safely and how to respond when hazards arise.
Core Safety Procedures
1. Hazard Communication & Right
to
Know
Hazard communication procedures ensure employees understand the chemical and physical hazards present in the workplace. Key elements include:
Proper labeling of hazardous materials using GHS standards
Accessible Safety Data Sheets (SDS) for all chemicals
Training on chemical handling, storage, and spill response
Clear signage for restricted areas, PPE zones, and hazard categories
Documentation of employee HazCom training
These procedures ensure compliance with OSHA’s Hazard Communication Standard and empower workers to recognize and avoid chemical risks.
2. Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)
LOTO procedures protect workers during maintenance, cleaning, or repair by ensuring machines remain fully de
energized. A compliant LOTO process includes:
Identifying all energy sources (electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, chemical)
Applying locks, hasps, and tags to isolation points
Verifying zero
energy state before work begins
Documenting authorized personnel and LOTO steps
Conducting annual audits of the LOTO program
LOTO prevents accidental startup and is one of the most critical safety procedures in manufacturing.
3. Machine Guarding Inspections
Routine inspections ensure machine guards and safety interlocks are functioning properly. Inspection procedures include:
Verifying guards are securely mounted and undamaged
Testing interlocks and presence
sensing devices
Checking for bypassed or removed guards
Documenting deficiencies and corrective actions
Scheduling preventive maintenance for safety components
These inspections prevent workers from contacting moving parts and ensure machines remain compliant with OSHA machine
guarding standards.
4. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Policies
PPE procedures define when, where, and how protective equipment must be used. Key elements include:
Issuing PPE appropriate for specific hazards
Inspecting PPE for wear, damage, or contamination
Enforcing PPE use in designated zones
Training employees on proper donning, doffing, and care
Maintaining replacement schedules and inventory controls
PPE procedures ensure workers have the correct protection as a final barrier against hazards.
5. Material Handling & Storage
Material handling procedures prevent injuries and ensure safe movement of goods throughout the facility. These include:
Safe lifting techniques and ergonomic practices
Rules for forklift, pallet jack, and crane operation
Proper stacking and storage of materials
Keeping walkways, exits, and emergency equipment unobstructed
Procedures for handling hazardous or fragile materials
These protocols reduce musculoskeletal injuries, struck
by incidents, and blocked egress hazards.
6. Ergonomics & Workstation Design
Ergonomic procedures minimize repetitive motion injuries and improve worker comfort. They include:
Adjusting workstation height, reach, and layout
Using mechanical aids for heavy lifting
Rotating jobs to reduce repetitive strain
Training employees on proper posture and movement
Conducting ergonomic assessments for high
risk tasks
These procedures help prevent sprains, strains, and long
term musculoskeletal disorders.
7. Emergency Response Planning
Emergency procedures ensure employees know how to respond during fires, spills, severe weather, or medical emergencies. Key components include:
Documented fire, chemical spill, and severe weather protocols
Clearly marked evacuation routes and exits
Routine emergency drills and alarm testing
Properly maintained extinguishers, eyewash stations, and spill kits
Communication plans for alerting employees during emergencies
These procedures protect workers and ensure regulatory compliance during critical events.
8. Routine Safety Audits & Incident Reporting
Audit and reporting procedures maintain continuous improvement and hazard awareness. They include:
Daily or weekly floor walkthroughs
Documentation of hazards, deficiencies, and corrective actions
Near
miss reporting systems
Incident investigation protocols
Encouraging a “see something, say something” culture
These procedures ensure hazards are identified early and addressed promptly.

This process is divided into three major phases:

 – Identify, analyze, and evaluate hazards

Step 1 — Identify Hazards
Identify all hazards associated with tasks, equipment, chemicals, and environments.
Sources of hazards include:
Mechanical (moving parts, pinch points, crush zones)
Electrical (arc flash, exposed conductors, faulty wiring)
Chemical (toxins, corrosives, flammables)
Environmental (noise, vibration, air quality, temperature)
Ergonomic (lifting, repetitive motion, awkward posture)
Process hazards (pressure, temperature, stored energy)
Human factors (fatigue, training gaps, behavior)
Tools used:
Floor inspections
Job Safety Analysis (JSA)
Safety Data Sheets (SDS)
Incident history
Observability sensors
Employee reports
Step 2 — Measure & Analyze Risk
Evaluate each hazard using structured methodologies.
Common methods:
RCA (Root Cause Analysis)
FMEA (Failure Mode & Effects Analysis)
5
Whys
Fishbone diagrams
Risk matrices (Severity × Likelihood)
Risk scoring example:
Severity: 1–5
Likelihood: 1–5
Risk Score = Severity × Likelihood
Risk Level: Low, Medium, High, Critical
Step 3 — Prioritize Risks
Rank hazards based on risk score and regulatory requirements.
Priority levels:
P1 – Critical
 (Immediate action required)
P2 – High
 (Action within 24–72 hours)
P3 – Medium
 (Action within 7–30 days)
P4 – Low
 (Monitor or schedule during routine maintenance)

Record all hazards, scores, and observations.
Documentation includes:
Hazard description
Location
Equipment involved
Photos or sensor data
Risk score
Recommended controls
Assigned personnel
Due dates
This becomes part of the facility’s permanent risk registry.
PHASE 2 — Risk Mitigation
Step 5 — Apply Control Measures (Hierarchy of Controls)
Controls must be applied in order of effectiveness:
Elimination
 – Remove the hazard entirely
Substitution
 – Replace hazardous materials or processes
Engineering Controls
 – Guards, interlocks, ventilation, automation
Administrative Controls
 – Policies, signage, scheduling, training
PPE
 – Final protective barrier
Your PlantSafety system can enforce this hierarchy by requiring justification when lower
level controls are used.
Step 6 — Implement Corrective Actions
Assign and execute mitigation tasks.

Install machine guards
Add light curtains or presence sensors
Improve ventilation or filtration
Update SOPs or training
Add ergonomic tools or lifting aids
Apply LOTO procedures
Modify workflows or shift schedules
Each corrective action must include:
Responsible person
Deadline
Verification steps
Evidence of completion
Step 7 — Validate Effectiveness
After mitigation, verify that the hazard is controlled.
Validation methods:
Follow
up inspections
Sensor data review
Employee feedback
Testing of guards/interlocks
Re
scoring the hazard
If risk remains unacceptable, additional controls must be applied.

Dashboard Design Architecture
The dashboard architecture is the 
visual and data aggregation layer
 of your PlantSafety platform. It sits on top of all modules (Risk, PPE, Observability, Permits, Scheduling, Alerts, etc.) and exposes their data as 
widgets
 (charts, graphs, tables, gauges, maps) organized into 
role
based dashboards
.
1. Architectural Layers
1. Data Sources (Domain Modules)
Risk Management, Incidents, PPE, Machines, Permits, Scheduling, Observability, Culture, Scorecards, Alerts, Training, Contractors, Checklists, Security, Environmental, Quality.
Each module exposes 
read models
 or 
query endpoints
 optimized for dashboard use.
2. Aggregation & Analytics Layer
ETL / Aggregation jobs
 (scheduled + event
driven) compute KPIs: TRIR, DART, risk scores, compliance rates, trends.
Time
series storage
 for sensor and observability data.
Pre
computed views
 for heavy queries (monthly trends, heatmaps, risk matrices).
Optional 
analytics engine
 for predictive risk and culture scoring.
3. Dashboard Service Layer
Central 
Dashboard API
 that:
Resolves which widgets belong to which dashboard.
Pulls data from aggregation/read models.
Applies 
role
based filters
 (plant, line, department, shift).
Supports 
widget configuration
 (time range, filters, thresholds).
4. Presentation Layer (UI)
Web UI with 
dashboard pages
, each composed of widgets.
Responsive layout: grid system for charts, tables, gauges, maps.
Real
time updates via 
WebSockets / SignalR
 for alerts and observability.
2. Core Concepts
1. Dashboard
Logical page (e.g., “Executive Safety Overview”, “Risk Management”, “PPE Compliance”).
Has: Id, Name, Description, Role, DefaultFilters, WidgetLayout.
2. Widget
Single visualization: chart, graph, gauge, table, heatmap, map, timeline.
Has:
WidgetId
Type (LineChart, BarChart, PieChart, Gauge, Table, Heatmap, Map, Timeline)
DataSource (query or endpoint)
Parameters (time range, plant, module, thresholds)
RefreshInterval
Permissions (who can see it)
3. Data Model for Widgets
Input:
 normalized series or tabular data:
Series[] (name, values, timestamps)
Categories[]
Metrics[] (value, label, severity)
Output:
 rendered visualization in UI.
3. Data Flow
Domain Events & Sensor Data
Incidents, near
misses, LOTO events, training completions, alerts, sensor readings.
Aggregation Jobs
Compute TRIR/DART, risk scores, compliance percentages, trends, heatmaps.
Store in 
aggregated tables
 or 
materialized views
.
Dashboard API Queries
Widgets call GET /dashboard/{id}/widgets or GET /widget/{id}/data.
API resolves data from aggregated views + live feeds (alerts, observability).
UI Rendering
Frontend maps widget type → visualization component.
Applies filters (plant, line, time range) and refresh intervals.

Important Clarification — Features Audit, Not Application Audit

 The

Glossary
A unified glossary of all safety, operational, and technical terms used throughout the PlantSafety program.
A
Audit
 — A systematic evaluation of operations, systems, procedures, or compliance. 
Alert
 — A real
time notification of a hazard, emergency, or operational deviation. 
Atmospheric Testing
 — Measurement of oxygen, toxic gases, and flammables in confined spaces.
B
Behavioral Observation
 — A documented review of worker actions to identify safe or unsafe behaviors. 
Bump Cap
 — Lightweight head protection for low
impact hazards.
C
Confined Space
 — An area with limited entry/exit not designed for continuous occupancy. 
Corrective Action
 — A task assigned to eliminate or mitigate a hazard or non
compliance. 
Culture Score
 — A metric representing the strength of the plant’s safety culture.
D
DART Rate
 — Days Away, Restricted, or Transferred injury rate. 
Dashboard
 — A visual interface displaying KPIs, charts, and safety metrics.
E
E
Stop (Emergency Stop)
 — A device that immediately halts machine operation. 
Environmental Monitoring
 — Tracking air quality, noise, temperature, and chemical exposure.
F
Fall Protection
 — Equipment preventing falls from heights (harnesses, lanyards, SRLs). 
FMEA
 — Failure Mode and Effects Analysis, used for risk assessment.
G
GHS Label
 — Globally Harmonized System label for chemical hazard communication. 
Gauge
 — A dashboard widget showing a single metric (e.g., compliance %).
H
Hazard
 — Any source of potential injury or harm. 
Heatmap
 — A visual map showing intensity or frequency of hazards or events. 
Hot Work
 — Work that produces sparks, flames, or heat.
I
Incident
 — An unplanned event causing injury, damage, or disruption. 
Interlock
 — A safety device preventing machine operation under unsafe conditions.
L
LOTO (Lockout/Tagout)
 — Procedures ensuring machines remain de
energized during maintenance. 
Lagging Indicator
 — A metric based on past incidents (TRIR, DART).
M
Man
Down Alert
 — Automated signal indicating a worker collapse or distress. 
Machine Guarding
 — Physical barriers preventing contact with hazardous machine parts.
N
Near
Miss
 — An event that could have caused injury but did not. 
Noise Exposure
 — Measurement of sound levels affecting hearing safety.
O
Observability
 — Real
time monitoring using sensors, AI, and IoT. 
Operational Audit
 — Review of production workflows and operator compliance.
P
PPE
 — Personal Protective Equipment. 
Permit
to
Work (PTW)
 — Formal authorization for high
risk tasks. 
Predictive Maintenance
 — Using sensor data to forecast equipment failure.
R
Risk Score
 — Numerical value representing hazard severity and likelihood. 
Root Cause Analysis (RCA)
 — Investigation method to determine underlying causes of incidents.
S
Scorecard
 — A dashboard summarizing leading and lagging safety indicators. 
Sensor
 — Device measuring environmental or machine conditions. 
Stop
Work Authority
 — Employee right to halt unsafe operations.
T
TRIR
 — Total Recordable Incident Rate. 
Timeline
 — Dashboard widget showing events over time.
W
Work Order
 — A task assigned for maintenance, corrective action, or inspection. 
Workstation Ergonomics
 — Design of tasks and equipment to reduce strain and injury.
